The test flight in 3D

Two photographs of the same ground, taken a few seconds apart from a moving aircraft. Once each camera's position and rotation are known, every point seen in both can be located in space.

Exterior orientation

The exterior orientation of a photograph is six numbers: the ground coordinates of the projection centre (X₀, Y₀, Z₀) and three rotation angles (ω, φ, κ). The angles build a rotation matrix R, which turns a direction measured in the camera into a direction on the ground. With it, the collinearity condition from Chapter 1 becomes a pair of equations:

x=−fr11(X−X0)+r21(Y−Y0)+r31(Z−Z0)r13(X−X0)+r23(Y−Y0)+r33(Z−Z0)y=−fr12(X−X0)+r22(Y−Y0)+r32(Z−Z0)r13(X−X0)+r23(Y−Y0)+r33(Z−Z0)

These are the collinearity equations. Read forward, they predict where a ground point appears in the photo. Read backward, an image point gives a ray, and the ray meets the ground somewhere along its length. With only one photo you need the terrain height to know where; with two photos, the second ray answers that question.

Overlap and the air base

Survey flights are planned so that consecutive photos overlap by about 60 percent along the flight line. Every point in the overlap is seen from two positions, and that is what makes three-dimensional measurement possible. The distance between the two exposure stations is the air base B.

The ratio of base to flying height, B/H, controls how precisely heights can be measured. A larger base means the two rays meet at a wider angle, so a small error in an image measurement moves the intersection point less. The sample pair has a B/H of about 0.5 and a forward overlap of about 70 percent, both typical for a wide-angle survey camera.
